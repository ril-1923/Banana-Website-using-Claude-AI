Art & Pop Culture

When Warhol Peeled Open the World

In 1967, Andy Warhol designed a banana for the cover of The Velvet Underground's debut album. The instruction on the original sleeve read "Peel slowly and see" — revealing a flesh-pink banana beneath. That single image transformed a humble fruit into an icon of avant-garde art, forever cementing the banana's place in cultural history. The image endures as one of the most recognisable album covers ever printed.

Political Economy

The Banana Republics: Power, Fruit & Empire

The term "Banana Republic" — coined by American author O. Henry in 1904 — describes nations whose governments were effectively controlled by foreign fruit corporations. The United Fruit Company (now Chiquita) wielded extraordinary political power across Central America, funding coups, manipulating governments, and controlling vast swaths of land and infrastructure. The 1954 Guatemalan coup d'état, orchestrated partly at the company's behest, remains one of history's most dramatic examples of corporate political power. The banana's sweetness masked a bitter political legacy that shaped an entire region.

Further Reading
I
The Gros Michel Extinction

Before the Cavendish, the world ate the Gros Michel — a creamier, richer variety wiped out by Panama disease in the 1950s. Scientists warn history may repeat itself.

II
Sacred Fruit of the Tropics

In Hindu tradition, banana plants are considered sacred to Lord Vishnu. Banana leaves serve as plates during ceremonies; the fruit features in every auspicious ritual.

III
The Slip Joke's Long History

The banana peel pratfall is one of comedy's oldest gags, documented in newspapers as early as 1872. Slapstick owes much of its legacy to this humble fruit.

Short Takes
IV
Banana Leaf Dining

Across South and Southeast Asia, banana leaves serve as biodegradable plates for elaborate meals. The leaf imparts a subtle flavour and carries antimicrobial properties.

V
The €120,000 Banana

In 2019, Maurizio Cattelan duct-taped a banana to a wall at Art Basel Miami and titled it "Comedian." It sold for $120,000 — and was later eaten by another artist.

VI
Banana Fibre Fashion

Banana stem fibres are woven into luxurious textiles in Japan (Kijoka-bashōfu) and the Philippines (t'nalak). Designers increasingly champion these as sustainable luxury materials.

Bananas
Around the World

West Africa
The Plantain Heartland

Plantains are a staple starch across Nigeria, Ghana, and Cameroon — fried as kelewele, boiled with peanut soup, or dried into chips. Cuisine here is unimaginable without them.

South India
Sacred & Culinary

Tamil Nadu's banana leaf meal (sadhya) is a ritual as much as a meal. Kerala produces hundreds of banana varieties; the Nendran plantain is prized for its chips and halwa.

Central America
Industrial Origin

Guatemala, Honduras, and Costa Rica supply much of the world's Cavendish. The landscape — and political history — of these nations was literally shaped by banana cultivation.

Southeast Asia
1,000 Varieties

The Philippines, Indonesia, and Thailand host extraordinary banana biodiversity. Saba, Lakatan, and Señorita varieties each carry distinct flavours, uses, and cultural meanings.

Editor's Note

There is no fruit quite like the banana. It has nourished empires and toppled governments; it has graced the walls of the world's most prestigious galleries and been eaten roadside for a few cents. It wraps itself in the symbolic language of both comedy and tragedy.

"The banana is at once the world's most humble fruit and its most consequential."

To study the banana is to study humanity itself — our migrations, our hungers, our politics, our art, and our capacity to find meaning in the most ordinary things. From the hills of Papua New Guinea to the stages of Carnegie Hall, the banana endures. Yellow, curved, improbable — and utterly irreplaceable.
